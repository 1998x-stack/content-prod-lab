# Geopolitical methodology

## Core model

Treat geopolitical outcomes as a function of interacting variables rather than one master cause:

`geography + relative power + economic networks + domestic politics + identity/norms + technology + time`

This is an analytical scaffold, not a mathematical law.

## 1. Geography

Ask:

- Why here?
- What does the terrain enable or constrain?
- Which ports, straits, corridors, islands, plains, rivers, bases, industrial zones, or resource areas matter?
- How does distance affect usable power?
- Has technology changed the value of the geography?

Avoid geographic determinism.

## 2. Classical geopolitical lenses

### Mahan

Useful for sea power, maritime commerce, naval bases, sea lines of communication, ports, and access to oceans.

Do not reduce Mahan to fleet size.

### Mackinder

Useful for continental scale, strategic depth, land communications, and the interaction between transport technology and Eurasian power.

Use cautiously; test whether air power, missiles, space systems, logistics, and economic networks have changed the assumptions.

### Spykman

Useful for rimland competition, coastal industrial belts, alliances, and the interaction of continental and maritime powers.

Especially useful where sea and land power meet.

## 3. Realist lenses

### Defensive realism

Ask whether actors are seeking adequate security and whether overexpansion creates balancing.

### Offensive realism

Ask whether uncertainty about intentions incentivizes powers to maximize relative position and regional influence.

### Neoclassical realism

Use when system-level pressure alone cannot explain timing or policy choice.

Test:

- leader perception,
- state capacity,
- institutions,
- domestic coalitions,
- extraction and mobilization ability.

### Security dilemma

Test whether one actor’s defensive step reasonably appears offensive to another, generating reciprocal insecurity.

Do not use the term to erase aggression or responsibility; it explains interaction dynamics, not legal equivalence.

## 4. Liberal / institutional lenses

Use for:

- institutions,
- alliance rules,
- trade dependence,
- cooperation under competition,
- domestic preferences,
- transaction costs and credible commitments.

Ask what institutions can constrain, enable, or coordinate — and where power overrides rules.

## 5. Geoeconomics

Map strategic dependencies across:

- energy,
- finance,
- trade,
- shipping,
- infrastructure,
- semiconductors,
- critical minerals,
- technology standards,
- payment systems,
- logistics and insurance.

Use the vulnerability heuristic:

`dependence x concentration x substitution difficulty x substitution time`

Ask: if the relationship is interrupted, who suffers more, how quickly, and who has substitutes?

## 6. Constructivism

Use when identity, norms, historical memory, prestige, status, or threat perception help explain why materially similar conditions produce different behavior.

Ask:

- Who is treated as friend, rival, or enemy?
- Which historical narratives define interests?
- Which norms constrain acceptable action?

## 7. Critical geopolitics

Examine categories and narratives such as “West”, “Global South”, “Indo-Pacific”, “historical territory”, “rules-based order”, or “strategic autonomy”.

Ask:

- Who uses the term?
- What space does it create?
- Who is included/excluded?
- Which policy becomes easier to justify once the map is framed this way?

Distinguish formal, practical, and popular geopolitics where useful.

## 8. Domestic politics

Do not model the state as a single mind.

Separate:

- state interest,
- regime interest,
- leader incentives,
- military/bureaucratic interests,
- business and regional interests,
- public opinion and electoral constraints.

## 9. Alliance analysis

Test:

- shared threat,
- burden sharing,
- interoperability,
- nuclear guarantees,
- basing,
- weapons and intelligence flows,
- abandonment fear,
- entrapment fear,
- free-riding and internal divergence.

## 10. Strategic layers

Always distinguish:

- tactical: immediate engagement-level result,
- operational: campaign/theater effect,
- strategic: effect on war or contest outcome,
- grand strategic: effect on long-term national position.

## 11. Second-order effects

For every major policy or event, ask:

- What does the opponent do next?
- What do allies do?
- What happens to markets, domestic politics, and neutral states?
- Does the action create blowback that offsets the direct gain?

## 12. Time horizons

When appropriate analyze:

- short: 0–12 months,
- medium: 1–5 years,
- long: 5–20 years.

Do not linearly extrapolate current trends into the long term.

## 13. Scenario method

Each scenario needs:

- trigger,
- mechanism,
- observable indicators,
- implications.

Scenarios are conditional pathways, not predictions written as certainties.

## 14. Red-team method

Attack the preferred conclusion by testing:

- alternative actor motives,
- measurement errors,
- hidden constraints,
- technology shifts,
- domestic political discontinuities,
- alliance changes,
- economic substitution,
- incorrect assumptions about willingness to bear costs.

A strong conclusion states what would prove it wrong.
