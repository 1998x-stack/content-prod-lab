# Research and sourcing protocol

## Source hierarchy

Use the strongest available source for each claim.

### Tier 1 — Primary / official

Prefer for formal positions, laws, treaties, statistics, budgets, sanctions, election or institutional facts:

- governments and ministries,
- international organizations,
- treaty texts and official legal documents,
- official statistical agencies,
- central banks,
- NATO, EU, UN, IMF, World Bank, IAEA, WTO and comparable bodies.

Primary sources are authoritative about **what the institution says or records**, not automatically about disputed causal claims.

### Tier 2 — High-quality newswires and major reporting

Prefer Reuters, AP, AFP and other reputable outlets for fast-moving factual events, especially where they independently verify claims.

### Tier 3 — Specialist research institutes

Use institutions such as IISS, SIPRI, RUSI, RAND, CSIS, Chatham House, Carnegie, CFR, ECFR, Brookings, and comparable regional institutes for synthesis, force structure, policy analysis, and interpretation.

Treat think-tank conclusions as analysis, not neutral fact; note institutional context when material.

### Tier 4 — Academic literature

Use peer-reviewed scholarship and academic presses for stable theory, historical interpretation, and methodological grounding.

## Search sequence

1. Establish chronology from primary and high-quality reporting.
2. Verify territorial, economic, military, demographic, and legal claims separately.
3. Search for official positions of all principal actors.
4. Search for high-quality independent analysis.
5. For contested issues, deliberately seek credible alternative interpretations.
6. Stop collecting when additional sources no longer change the factual model or major interpretations.

## Evidence labels

Use four categories internally and reflect them in prose where useful:

- **Confirmed fact** — supported by multiple reliable or direct sources.
- **Contested fact** — credible sources disagree or independent verification is limited.
- **Actor claim** — an official or interested party says this; do not rewrite as fact.
- **Analytical inference** — reasoned conclusion from evidence; explicitly label when load-bearing.

## Currentness

For current topics, check publication date and the date the event actually occurred.

When the user says “today”, “current”, “latest”, “now”, or asks about a current conflict, do not rely on memory alone.

## Numbers

For important statistics:

- prefer the original dataset,
- state the unit and period,
- note methodological differences when estimates vary,
- avoid spurious precision,
- do not average incompatible estimates merely to manufacture a single figure.

## Maps and territorial control

Treat control maps as estimates unless the underlying source establishes official borders or legally recognized status.

Always distinguish:

- de facto control,
- internationally recognized sovereignty,
- claimed territory.

## Legal and political distinction

Do not infer legality from strategic logic.

When international law matters, use treaty texts, UN resolutions, court decisions, or qualified legal scholarship rather than geopolitical commentary alone.

## Citation practice for the final article

Keep the article readable.

- Use inline source references or Word footnotes/endnotes where appropriate.
- Do not paste long raw URLs into body text.
- End with a curated “参考资料与延伸阅读” list, usually 10–20 high-value sources for a deep article.
- Preserve clickable hyperlinks in Word.
- Never expose internal tool citation tokens in the document.
